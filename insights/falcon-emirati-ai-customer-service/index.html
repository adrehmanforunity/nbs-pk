Falcon-Emirati AI for UAE customer service: what to test before a pilot

New Emirati Arabic language and speech models create an opportunity to evaluate more local-language customer experiences. A launch announcement or benchmark is only a starting point: contact centres need evidence from their own dialects, calls, service tasks, and escalation paths.

By National Business Services · Published 2 November 2026

On 6 October 2026, Abu Dhabi's Technology Innovation Institute (TII) announced Falcon-Emirati, a language model developed for Emirati Arabic, alongside Falcon-ASR for speech transcription and Falcon-OCR-Arabic for extracting Arabic text from images and documents. The launch is relevant to organisations exploring Arabic-language AI, but it does not by itself establish that any model is ready for a live contact-centre workflow.

It also helps to distinguish the components. Falcon-ASR converts speech into text; a language model or service application may then interpret the request and produce a response. Each step can introduce different errors. Evaluate the complete journey, not just how fluent a demo sounds.

What the launch tells us - and what it does not

TII describes Falcon-Emirati as a 7-billion-parameter model built for Emirati vocabulary, expressions, and cultural context. It reports an 84.83% result on Alyah, a benchmark it describes as covering everyday language, figurative expressions, heritage knowledge, and poetry. TII also describes Falcon-ASR as supporting spoken Emirati and Modern Standard Arabic, as well as English, French, Spanish, and Portuguese.

These are details in the model developer's announcement, not independent evidence of accuracy for your customers, service intents, audio conditions, or integrations. A benchmark score is not a forecast of call resolution, agent workload, or customer satisfaction. Confirm model access, licensing, deployment options, data handling, and current capabilities directly with the provider before considering a pilot.

Build a small, realistic test set

Start with one bounded service task and define what a correct, safe outcome looks like. Use examples reviewed by fluent speakers familiar with the intended UAE audience. Include:

  • Emirati Arabic speech and the formal Arabic or other languages your customers actually use.
  • Natural code-switching, varied speaking rates, interruptions, background noise, and different audio quality.
  • Local names, place names, product terms, dates, amounts, account references, and negation.
  • Common requests as well as complaints, ambiguous questions, corrections, and out-of-scope requests.
  • Cases where a customer asks for a person, disputes an answer, or needs an action the AI must not take.

Use synthetic or appropriately de-identified examples where possible. Do not reuse customer recordings or personal information without the necessary authorization, notice, access controls, retention rules, and privacy review.

Score transcription, understanding, and service separately

Keep an error log for each stage so that a plausible-sounding response does not hide a misheard request. Ask qualified reviewers to check whether:

  • Transcription: Speech recognition preserves names, numbers, negation, and service-critical details.
  • Understanding: The application identifies the customer's intent and the information needed to proceed.
  • Response: The answer is grounded in approved information and is understandable and appropriate for the intended audience.
  • Action: The system only performs actions it is authorized to perform and confirms important changes.
  • Recovery: It asks a useful clarification question or hands off when the request is unclear, sensitive, or unsupported.

Report results by task and error type as well as in aggregate. A strong overall average can conceal serious mistakes with amounts, identity details, complaints, or one group of speakers. Set acceptance criteria before the test and decide in advance which errors require immediate human handling.

Test the human handoff and operating model

A language model does not replace the rest of the customer-service system. Check how it retrieves approved knowledge, what context reaches the agent, whether the customer has to repeat information, and what happens if a model, connection, or downstream system is unavailable. Test with your intended channels and integrations; do not infer production readiness from a standalone chat demonstration.

During an initial pilot, keep the scope narrow, monitor outcomes, and provide a clear route to a trained employee. Track resolution quality, repeat contacts, transfers, correction rates, and consequential errors - not only response speed or the number of automated conversations.

Questions to ask before choosing a pilot

  • Can the provider demonstrate performance on approved examples representative of our Emirati Arabic use case?
  • Which model handles transcription, which handles language understanding, and where does each run?
  • How are prompts, audio, transcripts, and customer data stored, accessed, retained, and deleted?
  • What are the licensing, usage, integration, monitoring, and human-review costs?
  • How are model updates announced, and how will we detect regressions before they affect customers?
  • Can we pause automation and route work to a person if quality or availability falls below our agreed threshold?

Bottom line

Falcon-Emirati and Falcon-ASR make a timely case for testing locally relevant Arabic AI, but the business decision should rest on a controlled evaluation - not a headline score or the assumption that Emirati Arabic represents every Arabic-speaking customer. Start with a low-risk task, measure each stage, protect customer data, and retain human oversight until the evidence supports a wider role.
